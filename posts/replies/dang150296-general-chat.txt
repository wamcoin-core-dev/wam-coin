--------------------------------------------------------------- 1 of 5

A few people are reading the Gleec swap as a price. It is worth saying
exactly what happened, because the whole thing is public and anyone can check
it line by line.

A maintainer at Gleec asked for some WAM so he could test that swaps work at
all -- somebody holding none of a coin cannot trade it against anything. We
sent it. He then set the rate himself and ran the trade. We were not asked for
a number and we did not suggest one. The entire exchange is a public pull
request: GLEECBTC/coins#2034. Open it and read every line between us.

The 500 WAM is recorded in docs/TREASURY_LEDGER.md with its transaction id,
and you can check it against your own node rather than believe it. No listing
fee was asked for and none was paid.

--------------------------------------------------------------- 2 of 5

On who sets a price: not us.

A founder who announces what his coin is worth is telling you the number is
his to decide, and if it is his to decide then it is his to change. That is
the opposite of what this project is for. The price of WAM is whatever two
people who do not know each other agree on, and nothing said in this channel
by me or anyone else makes it a different number.

This is not modesty and it is not a dodge. It is what decentralisation means
when it costs something. Anyone can run a node. Anyone can mine. Anyone can
read the ledger. The same rule that stops me from printing coins stops me from
setting a price, and a project that would break the second one has already
told you what it would do with the first.

--------------------------------------------------------------- 3 of 5

On what WAM actually is, since it keeps being asked:

It is Bitcoin's code and Bitcoin's economics -- a fixed cap of 22,000,000, a
halving, one transparent ledger anyone can audit from their own node -- with
the proof of work swapped for RandomX, which is Monero's. The lineage is
borrowed rather than invented, and saying so is more useful than a slogan.

What that changes is who gets to take part. Nobody mines Bitcoin on a home
machine any more. On WAM the computer you are reading this on is a real miner
today, and the network is still small enough that one person's machine moves
it. RandomX is Monero's work and CPU mining is not our idea -- what differs
there is that WAM's ledger is open where Monero's is private by design, and
WAM's supply ends where Monero's emits forever. Different answers to
different problems, not a better version of either.

--------------------------------------------------------------- 4 of 5

On what gives a coin a reason to exist beyond being mined and sold -- that was
the real question, and there is one answer I can point at instead of promise.

A security reviewer read this code, found real problems, and agreed to be paid
in WAM rather than in dollars or bitcoin. He is in this channel, so that is
checkable rather than a claim. That is the coin being used as money for work
that had a price, and nobody arranged it to look good.

It is owed and it is not yet paid. When it is paid, the amount and the
transaction id go on the public record the same way the 500 WAM did. Two
accepted findings are listed in SECURITY.md with their amounts and the reason
for each amount, including the one where we argued ourselves down to a lower
tier and wrote out why.

HIS FOLLOW-UP: how does an idea become part of WAM?
=====================================================================

Asked 2026-09-23 in #general-chat: the path from proposal to discussion to
decision to funding to implementation; whether there will be a formal
process, treasury funding, contributor bounties, maintainer review; and
whether WAM develops its own technical identity or keeps the base protocol
simple and lets the ecosystem differentiate around it.

--------------------------------------------------------------- 6 of 8

How an idea becomes part of WAM, mechanically:

It arrives as an issue or a pull request, in the open. It gets argued about
by whoever shows up. If it survives that, it ships in a release. And then the
part that most projects do not have -- nobody has to take it. A release is an
offer, not an instruction. Every node operator chooses whether to run it, and
a change that people do not install does not happen, no matter who wrote it,
who merged it, or whose key signed the binary.

That is worth being precise about, because it is the whole answer to "who
decides". There is no board to lobby, no token weight to accumulate, no
foundation that can be pressured or bought, and no seat I could hand someone
even if I wanted to. The people running the software decide, by running it or
not. It is a slower mechanism than a vote and a much harder one to capture,
because there is nothing in the middle to capture.

What is not written down yet is the path in front of that: how a proposal
gets discussed, who reviews it, how it gets funded if it needs funding. That
document does not exist, and I would rather say so than describe one.

--------------------------------------------------------------- 7 of 8

What exists today, each of which you can check rather than take from me:

  * Bounties, published before launch with their tiers, in SECURITY.md. Two
    findings are accepted and listed there with the amount and the reasoning
    for the amount -- including the one where we argued ourselves DOWN to a
    lower tier and wrote out why, because a tier decision without a reason is
    just a number.

  * A treasury with a stated purpose and a hard end. 5% of the block subsidy,
    heights 1 to 400,000, then zero -- about eighteen months and 750,000 WAM
    in total, ever. The whitepaper section 3.2 names what it funds: listings,
    an independent audit, infrastructure, legal costs, and development. It is
    working capital for a launch, not a perpetual fund, and pretending
    otherwise would set up exactly the disappointment you are asking about.

  * Every coin that leaves it published with its amount, its reason and its
    transaction id, checkable against your own node.

What does not exist: a written proposal process. Not a review culture --
outside reviewers have read this code and their findings changed it, two of
them accepted and listed in SECURITY.md with the amounts, and friends read it
before any of that. What is missing is the written path from an idea to a
decision, which is the thing you actually asked about.

A governance document written alone by the person it is meant to constrain is
not worth the file it sits in. If this matters to you, help write it -- that
is a real invitation and not a polite one.

--------------------------------------------------------------- 8 of 8

Your last question is the one I have an actual opinion on.

The base layer should stay conservative, and that IS the technical identity
rather than the absence of one. The consensus rules are the terms every person
who pointed a machine at this chain agreed to: a 22,000,000 cap, a halving
every 200,000 blocks, a premine vested to 2031 and not liquid at launch, and a
treasury fee that expires by consensus at height 400,000. CONTRIBUTING.md
already puts changes to those out of scope, and it said so before there was
anyone to say it to. A patch that alters them is a proposal for a different
coin, and it should be honest enough to call itself one.

What that leaves is not nothing -- it is most of the work. The node, the
reference miner, the pool, the explorer, wallets, payment tooling, everything
that makes a coin usable rather than merely valid. That is where differences
between projects actually get made, and none of it needs permission from a
consensus rule.

So: a small, hard, boring base, and everything interesting built around it.
That is Bitcoin's bet, and choosing it deliberately after seeing the
alternatives is a position, not a lack of one.
